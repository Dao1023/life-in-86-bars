If you’re like most people, your income and spending changes significantly as you get older.

In the most common career trajectory, earlier years coincide with a lower salary as skills and experience grow. Then, peak earning years are achieved in late adulthood, and eventually retirement comes onto the horizon.

Is this typical earnings arc supported by data?

## Income and Spending, by Age

The data visualizations in today’s post come to us from [Engaging Data](https://engaging-data.com/household-spending-age/) and they use Sankey diagrams to display data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) showing differences in how various age groups in America earn and spend their money.

The four charts below will show household data based on the age of the primary resident:

1.   Less than 25 years old (Very early career)
2.   Between 25-34 years old (Early career)
3.   Between 45-54 years old (Peak earning and spending)
4.   More than 75 years old (Retirement)

    
Let’s take a look at the collection of data, to see how it shakes out.

    
#### Less than 25 Years Old – $31,102 in spending (94.6%% of total income)

    
_These contain an average of 1.9 people (1.3 income earners, 0.3 children, and 0.0 seniors)_  
![Image 86: Less than 25 Years Old](https://www.visualcapitalist.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/spending-by-age-25-years.jpg)

    
For the average household with a primary resident under 25 years old, total income is $32,893.

    
The biggest expense is housing (24.3% of spending), followed by vehicles (10.8%), gas and insurance (9.3%), food at home (7.7%), and dining out (7.6%). For this younger cohort, education is also a significant expense at $2,333 per year (7.5% of spending).

    
#### Between 25-34 Years – $48,928 in spending (70.8% of total income)

    
_These contain an average of 2.8 people (1.5 income earners, 1.0 children, and 0.0 seniors)_  
![Image 87: Between 25-34 Years Old](https://www.visualcapitalist.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/spending-by-age-34-years.jpg)

    
In this age range, earning potential starts to rapidly expand with experience – and households make double that of the previous category (Under 25 years old).

    
Housing remains the biggest expense (25.9% of spending), followed by gas and insurance (9.2%), household expenses (8.2%), food at home (8.1%), and then vehicles (8.1%).

    
#### Between 45-54 Years – $64,781 in spending (64.6% of total income)

    
_These contain an average of 2.8 people (1.7 income earners, 0.7 children, and 0.1 seniors)_  
![Image 88: Between 45-54 Years Old](https://www.visualcapitalist.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/spending-by-age-54-years.jpg)

    
This age range is notable because it has both the highest income and the highest spending. It also represents a time of peak savings, with the average household stashing away $19,159 per year.

    
Expenses are similar to the previous category. Housing is the biggest expense (22.0%), followed by gas and insurance (9.0%), food at home (7.9%), vehicles (7.9%), and household expenses (6.7%).

    
#### Over 75 Years Old – $40,211 in spending (95.6% of total income)

    
_These contain an average of 2.6 people (0.2 income earners, 0.0 children, and 1.4 seniors)_  
![Image 89: Over 75 Years Old](https://www.visualcapitalist.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/spending-by-age-75-years.jpg)

    
Not surprisingly, here we see salary contributing just $7,891 per year to total income, with social security supplementing income with $25,057 per year.

    
For this older segment, health insurance (8.2%) jumps up to become the second most important expense. Meanwhile, driving and housing both drop in their respective allocations.

    
## The Typical Earning Arc?

    
The data confirms that conventional wisdom around the typical earning trajectory for Americans seems pretty accurate.

## Why American Salaries Go Further Than Canadian Salaries

The purchasing power gap between the U.S. and Canada is particularly striking given the relatively high living costs in both countries.

The difference is reinforced by wage data. In 2025, average annual wages reached [$86,977](https://www.oecd.org/en/data/indicators/average-annual-wages.html) in the U.S., compared with $67,901 in Canada, after adjusting for differences in local prices. That’s a 28% advantage for American workers.

Although these figures represent gross wages while Numbeo uses take-home pay, both comparisons point to a similar conclusion: the U.S. wage advantage persists even after accounting for differences in living costs.

## Why Cuba Has the World’s Weakest Purchasing Power

In 2025, the average monthly state salary in Cuba was 6,930 Cuban pesos, equivalent to roughly $13 at the informal exchange rate reported in April 2026.

The country’s state-dominated wage system and depreciating currency have severely weakened household purchasing power. Food shortages, shrinking subsidized rations, and frequent power outages have compounded the pressure on households.

## Why High Living Costs Don’t Always Mean Less Buying Power

Switzerland ranks third worldwide, despite being one of Europe’s [most expensive](https://www.visualcapitalist.com/worlds-most-expensive-cities-to-live-in-2025/) countries. Portugal, where living costs are considerably lower, ranks 54th. Average salaries in Switzerland offer roughly 2.5 times the purchasing power of those in Portugal.

Oman offers another surprising example. Ranking fifth globally, ahead of the U.S., Denmark, and Australia, its strong salaries, absence of personal income tax, and relatively low living costs help explain why paychecks stretch further in the oil-rich economy.

These comparisons highlight why affordability depends on both earnings and expenses. Countries with high living costs can still offer strong purchasing power when local wages are high enough to offset them.

## What Higher Debt Means for Americans Today

The amount Americans owe tells only part of the story. Monthly payments, interest rates, and household income ultimately determine how manageable that debt is.
